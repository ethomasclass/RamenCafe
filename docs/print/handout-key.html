3Causes

IDENTIFYTwo causes of an aging population.

1. Falling fertility — Japan’s total fertility rate is about 1.2, below the replacement level of 2.1, so each generation is smaller than the one before.

2. Rising life expectancy — about 84 years. People are not dying at the ages they used to.

Accept “people having children later” as part of cause 1 if they explain that later first births compound into fewer births overall.

EXPLAINWhy one cause is “a triumph,” and why that makes it harder to talk about.

Longer life is a public-health success, not a failure — Etsuko says she has survived two things that would have killed her mother at her age. It is hard to discuss because the “problem” is partly caused by something nobody wants to reverse, so public argument displaces onto the half that can be blamed on somebody: young women’s fertility decisions. Full credit needs both halves — the success, and why naming it is awkward.

4Consequences

DESCRIBEThe old-age dependency ratio, using Daiki and Etsuko.

The number of people aged 65 and over for every 100 people of working age (15–64). Japan’s is about 51 — roughly two working-age adults per retired person, up from about 10 per 100 in 1970. In the shop: Etsuko is the one over 65, and of her two children only Daiki is close enough to drive her, so in practice the ratio at that counter is one to one.

Three things earn the mark: the definition, the characters, and a figure. A definition alone is a partial answer.

EXPLAINRen’s bus — the chain, and why it is a loop.

Fewer children born → the local school closes (it shut in 2016 with eleven pupils left) → young families will not move to or stay in a town where a six-year-old rides a bus 35 minutes each way.

Why it is a loop: the consequence becomes the cause. Every closure makes the next family’s decision to leave more rational, which produces fewer children, which closes the next service. Accept “feedback loop” or “vicious cycle”; the mechanism matters more than the term.

COMPAREKenji and Hiroshi.

Similarity: both are over 75, both have a child in a distant city who loves them and cannot visit — Kenji’s daughter in Tokyo, Hiroshi’s son in Chiba — and neither has daily family contact. Both depend on something other than family for routine company.

Difference: Kenji is embedded in the town he has lived in all his life and has paid daily care in Mary; his problem is that the town is emptying around him. Hiroshi is an incomer whose entire network was a company in Tokyo, has no paid carer and no local kin; his contact is a sensor and twenty minutes at a counter.

Concepts: Kenji → rural depopulation, silver economy, elder care outside the family. Hiroshi → social isolation, single-person households.

The distinction to push for here: Kenji is lonely because a place emptied; Hiroshi is lonely because a workplace ended. Students who see that these are two different mechanisms with the same surface have understood the topic better than students who wrote a longer answer about sadness.

5Policy

EXPLAINTwo things Aiko is weighing that ¥600,000 does not touch — and what that shows.

Any two of: childcare costs after the first year; her nine years of career and the risk shown by her team lead losing the Osaka account; no grandmother nearby (her own mother still works part-time at 62); rent and housing; the 35-minute school bus; that it is her career that pauses and not Ren’s; and Ren’s point that the childcare years and the eldercare years would be the same five years.

What it shows: pro-natalist cash addresses the smallest of the constraints. Money is not the binding one — time, career risk and missing infrastructure are — which is why big packages are estimated to move fertility by roughly +0.1 when the gap is about 0.9. Aiko’s own line is the target: she is not insulted that the sum is small, she is insulted that it assumes she did not want to.

IDENTIFY & EXPLAINTwo responses to a labor shortage, and a limit of each.

1. Immigration / foreign labor. Shown by Mary, in Japan on the care-worker visa route; the country is short roughly half a million care workers. Limit: the route is narrow and politically constrained (“I am a policy… it is a narrow door”), and paid care does not replace family — Kenji’s admission is precisely that.

2. Automation. Care robotics shipments up 19% a year; lifting frames, monitoring sensors, medication dispensers, and Hiroshi’s flask. Limit: the care-home manager’s line — “a lifting frame cannot hold a hand.” Machines cover tasks, not company.

Also creditable: raising wages. Mie raised hers 22% and got four applicants, two of them over 60 — which demonstrates the constraint is demographic rather than a price problem.

6The long answer — rubric

PtEarned when the response…
1Names a character and their situation using at least one specific detail from the game (a number, a quote, an object, a decision) rather than a summary.
1Correctly classifies the consequence as political, economic or social. Accept a defended second category — Mary is reasonably economic or social — if the defence is stated.
1Uses two concept-bank terms correctly in context. Naming a term without using it does not earn this point.
1Explains the causal link: how an aging population produces this person’s situation. There must be a mechanism — a because, or a chain of at least two steps.

A response that describes a character vividly but never connects them to the demographic cause caps at 2 of 4. That is the single most common outcome and it is worth saying so before they write.

Two marking notes.
1. Students will not all have the same evidence. Each customer has one admission they only make over a bowl that suited them, and the closing screen tells each student how many of the eight they missed. A student who heard three has less material than one who heard seven. This is by design — do not penalise thin detail that came from a bowl going wrong; ask them instead to get the missing one from somebody who heard it. That exchange is the discussion.
2. Detail is not the same as depth. The strongest papers are usually not the longest. Column 4 on page 1 and the fourth rubric point are the same skill, and it is the only one the FRQ actually rewards.
Answers that look right and are not: “This relates to the dependency ratio” — names a concept, supplies no mechanism, earns nothing. “Daiki is tired and stressed” — a summary, not a specific detail. “The shop closed because business was bad” — actively wrong; Kenji says it ran out of customers and out of a successor, which is the whole distinction between a failing business and a shrinking population. “Japan is unusual” — the end card names South Korea, Italy, Spain, Germany and China on the same road. Japan is early, not strange.
