## Page 1 — the five conversations

Every row has **several** right answers; these are the strongest. Accept any
specific detail that genuinely supports the concept the student named. Column 4
must contain a **mechanism** — a *because*, or a cause and an effect — not a
restatement.

### Daiki, 46
**Details:** drives Etsuko to Kōriyama twice a month, 50 minutes each way, and
takes the Thursday off · moved back from Osaka "because there wasn't anybody
else to" · had a team of eleven, now he drives · the town clinic closed in 2019
when its only doctor was 71.
**Concepts:** old-age dependency ratio; caregiving burden.
**Column 4 should say:** one person over 65 is supported by one working-age
adult in that family — his sister is in Sendai, so the physical work is his
alone. Nationally the ratio is about **51 per 100** (about 10 in 1970). The
point: it is not a statistic here, it is a number of hours and a forfeited
career. Over 100,000 people a year in Japan cut or leave work to care for a
parent.

### Kenji & Mary, 81 & 34
**Details:** Nakano's closed after 51 years — "ran out of customers and ran out
of a son" · eleven businesses on the street, now three · bus 9/day → 2 · school
shut in 2016 · town 4,000 → under 1,500, half over 65 · Mary came on the
care-worker visa route; Japan is short ~500,000 care workers.
**Concepts:** rural depopulation; silver economy; labor shortage;
immigration / foreign labor.
**Column 4 should say:** the shop did not fail commercially — the population
that supported it left or aged out, which is depopulation, not bad business.
The silver economy is real but needs density, so it concentrates in Kōriyama
and Sendai, and a shrinking town is a market with nobody selling to it. Mary is
in the room *because* the domestic workforce cannot fill care jobs.

### Aiko & Ren, 33 & 34
**Details:** ¥600,000 per child plus moving money and a daycare waiver · her
team lead came back from leave and lost the Osaka account · her mother had
three by 31, married at 23, with a grandmother four minutes away · the nearest
school is a 35-minute bus from age six.
**Concepts:** total fertility rate; replacement level; pro-natalist policy;
delayed childbearing.
**Column 4 should say:** Japan's TFR is about **1.2** against a replacement
level of **2.1**. The grant covers roughly one year of childcare against
eighteen years of cost and nine years of career — it does not touch hours,
housing, or whose career pauses. That is why decades of pro-natalist spending
have moved the rate very little (big packages ≈ **+0.1**; the gap ≈ **0.9**).

### Tanaka & Yui, 76 & 38
**Details:** one building budget — senior centre *or* extended-hours childcare ·
over-65 turnout ~4 in 5, twentysomethings ~1 in 3 · pensions and healthcare
~a third of the national budget, paid by people working now · ~4 in 10
municipalities at risk by 2040.
**Concepts:** voter turnout; pension burden; (silver democracy).
**Column 4 should say:** spending follows reliable voters. Over-65s are both
the larger bloc *and* the more reliable one, so senior services win without
anybody intending to neglect the young — Tanaka's line is that no councillor
ever says it, but every one of them can count. Second creditable mechanism:
there are simply fewer young voters, which is the birth rate arriving at a
ballot box twenty years late.

### Hiroshi, 79
**Details:** 41 years at one company in Tokyo — "every single person you know" ·
a flask that messages his son in Chiba when he makes tea · waited on the bench
outside so as not to be a nuisance · fell in March and worked out nobody would
know until Friday.
**Concepts:** social isolation; single-person households; (watch-over services,
silver economy).
**Column 4 should say:** his network *was* his employer, so retirement ended it
rather than shrinking it — and as an incomer with no local kin, nothing
replaced it. About a fifth of Japanese men over 65 live alone. With family four
hours away, routine contact has to be manufactured, by a sensor or a standing
Tuesday. Credit answers that treat isolation as a **structural outcome** rather
than a personality.

### Etsuko, 74
**Details:** "eleven working people for every two of my age … nearer two to one
now, and I am the one" · "we stopped dying and we did not start being born" ·
nine million empty houses, one of which will be this shop.
**Concepts:** life expectancy; old-age dependency ratio; natural decrease.
**Column 4 should say:** she supplies the cause students leave out. An aging
population is produced by falling births *and* rising life expectancy at once,
and the second is a public-health success. Look for students who can hold both.

## Page 2 — after the shop closes

### 3. Causes

**IDENTIFY — two causes.**
1. Falling fertility — TFR ≈ **1.2**, below the replacement level of **2.1**, so
   each generation is smaller than the last.
2. Rising life expectancy — about **84 years**.

*Accept "people having children later" as part of cause 1 if they explain that
later first births compound into fewer births overall.*

**EXPLAIN — why one cause is "a triumph."**
Longer life is a public-health success, not a failure — Etsuko has survived two
things that would have killed her mother at the same age. It is hard to discuss
because the "problem" is partly caused by something nobody wants to reverse, so
public argument displaces onto the half that can be blamed on somebody: young
women's fertility decisions. **Full credit needs both halves** — the success,
*and* why naming it is awkward.

### 4. Consequences

**DESCRIBE — the old-age dependency ratio.**
People aged **65+** for every **100 of working age (15–64)**. Japan's is about
**51** — roughly two working-age adults per retired person, up from about 10 per
100 in 1970. In the shop: Etsuko is the one over 65, and of her two children
only Daiki is close enough to drive her, so in practice the ratio at that
counter is one to one.
*Three things earn the mark: the definition, the characters, and a figure.*

**EXPLAIN — Ren's bus.**
Fewer children born → the local school closes (it shut in 2016 with eleven
pupils) → young families will not move to or stay in a town where a six-year-old
rides a bus 35 minutes each way.
**Why it is a loop:** the consequence becomes the cause. Every closure makes the
next family's decision to leave more rational, which produces fewer children,
which closes the next service. Accept "feedback loop" or "vicious cycle"; the
mechanism matters more than the term.

**COMPARE — Kenji and Hiroshi.**
- **Similarity:** both over 75, both with a child in a distant city who loves
  them and cannot visit (Tokyo; Chiba), neither with daily family contact. Both
  depend on something other than family for routine company.
- **Difference:** Kenji is embedded in the town he has lived in all his life and
  has *paid* daily care in Mary; his problem is that the town is emptying around
  him. Hiroshi is an incomer whose entire network was a company in Tokyo, has no
  paid carer and no local kin; his contact is a sensor and twenty minutes at a
  counter.
- **Concepts:** Kenji → rural depopulation, silver economy, elder care outside
  the family. Hiroshi → social isolation, single-person households.

> **The distinction to push for:** Kenji is lonely *because a place emptied*;
> Hiroshi is lonely *because a workplace ended*. Students who see that these are
> two different mechanisms with the same surface have understood the topic
> better than students who wrote a longer answer about sadness.

### 5. Policy

**EXPLAIN — two things ¥600,000 does not touch.**
Any two of: childcare costs after the first year; her nine years of career and
the risk shown by her team lead losing the Osaka account; no grandmother nearby
(her own mother still works part-time at 62); rent and housing; the 35-minute
school bus; that it is her career that pauses and not Ren's; Ren's point that
the childcare years and the eldercare years would be the *same five years*.
**What it shows:** pro-natalist cash addresses the smallest of the constraints.
Money is not the binding one — time, career risk and missing infrastructure are.
Aiko's own line is the target: she is not insulted that the sum is small, she is
insulted that it assumes she did not want to.

**IDENTIFY & EXPLAIN — two responses to a labor shortage.**
1. **Immigration / foreign labor.** Mary, on the care-worker visa route; Japan is
   short roughly half a million care workers. *Limit:* the route is narrow and
   politically constrained, and paid care does not replace family — Kenji's
   admission is precisely that.
2. **Automation.** Care robotics up 19% a year; lifting frames, monitoring
   sensors, medication dispensers, Hiroshi's flask. *Limit:* "a lifting frame
   cannot hold a hand." Machines cover tasks, not company.

*Also creditable: raising wages. Mie raised hers 22% and got four applicants,
two of them over 60 — which shows the constraint is demographic, not a price
problem.*

### 6. The long answer — rubric

| Pt | Earned when the response… |
|---|---|
| 1 | Names a character and their situation using at least one **specific** detail (a number, a quote, an object, a decision) rather than a summary |
| 1 | Correctly classifies the consequence as **political**, **economic** or **social**. Accept a defended second category — Mary is reasonably economic *or* social — if the defence is stated |
| 1 | Uses **two** concept-bank terms correctly in context. Naming a term without using it does not earn this point |
| 1 | Explains the **causal link**: how an aging population *produces* this person's situation. There must be a mechanism — a because, or a chain of at least two steps |

A response that describes a character vividly but never connects them to the
demographic cause caps at **2 of 4**. That is the single most common outcome and
it is worth saying so before they write.

## Two marking notes

**1. Students will not all have the same evidence.** Each customer has one
admission they only make over a bowl that suited them, and the closing screen
tells each student how many of the eight they missed. A student who heard three
has less material than one who heard seven. This is by design — do not penalise
thin detail that came from a bowl going wrong; ask them instead to get the
missing one from somebody who heard it. That exchange is the discussion.

**2. Detail is not the same as depth.** The strongest papers are usually not the
longest. Column 4 on page 1 and the fourth rubric point are the same skill, and
it is the only one the FRQ actually rewards.

## Answers that look right and are not

- **"This relates to the dependency ratio"** — names a concept, supplies no
  mechanism, earns nothing.
- **"Daiki is tired and stressed"** — a summary, not a specific detail.
- **"The shop closed because business was bad"** — actively wrong. Kenji says it
  ran out of *customers* and out of a *successor*, which is the whole
  distinction between a failing business and a shrinking population.
- **"Japan is unusual"** — the end card names South Korea, Italy, Spain, Germany
  and China on the same road. Japan is early, not strange.
